# MIT 18.06 Linear Algebra - Lecture 1: The Geometry of Linear Equations

## The Fundamental Problem of Linear Algebra

The fundamental problem of linear algebra is to solve a system of $n$ linear equations with $n$ unknowns. 

To understand these systems, we can look at them through three different perspectives:
1. **The Row Picture:** Examining one equation at a time to see where lines or planes intersect.
2. **The Column Picture:** Combining the columns of a matrix using linear combinations to produce a target vector.
3. **The Matrix Form:** Writing the system algebraically as $Ax = b$.

---

## Two Equations with Two Unknowns ($2 \times 2$)

Consider the system:
$$2x - y = 0$$
$$-x + 2y = 3$$

### 1. The Matrix Form ($Ax = b$)
The system can be written as:
$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

* **$A$** is the coefficient matrix: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$
* **$x$** is the vector of unknowns: $\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$
* **$b$** is the right-hand side vector: $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$

### 2. The Row Picture
In the $xy$-plane, each equation represents a straight line:
* The first line, $2x - y = 0$, passes through the origin $(0,0)$ and the point $(1,2)$.
* The second line, $-x + 2y = 3$, passes through $(-3,0)$ and $(-1,1)$.
* The solution is the intersection point of these two lines: $(1,2)$.

### 3. The Column Picture
Instead of lines, we look at the columns of matrix $A$ as vectors and find their **linear combination**:
$$x \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

* **Column 1 ($Col_1$):** $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$
* **Column 2 ($Col_2$):** $\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$

To find the solution geometrically, we combine these vectors:
* Take $1$ of $Col_1$ and $2$ of $Col_2$:
  $$1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 - 2 \\ -1 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
* Geometrically, placing $1 \cdot Col_1$ and then attaching $2 \cdot Col_2$ head-to-tail ends exactly at the vector $b = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

---

## Three Equations with Three Unknowns ($3 \times 3$)

Consider the system:
$$2x - y = 0$$
$$-x + 2y - z = -1$$
$$-3y + 4z = 4$$

### 1. The Row Picture
In three-dimensional space ($x, y, z$):
* Each of the three equations represents a flat plane.
* The first two planes intersect to form a line.
* The third plane intersects that line at a single point, which is the unique solution to the system.
* *Note:* The row picture becomes difficult to visualize in three dimensions and nearly impossible in higher dimensions.

### 2. The Column Picture
We express the system as a linear combination of three-dimensional column vectors:
$$x \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix} + z \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

* $x = 0$

Title: System Design for Beginners: A Step-by-Step Guide

# System Design for Beginners: A Step-by-Step Guide

System design is the skill of choosing the right technology components and placing them in the correct locations to solve a software problem. 

## 1. High-Level Design (HLD) vs. Low-Level Design (LLD)

*   **High-Level Design (HLD):** The zoomed-out blueprint of a system. It focuses on major components (servers, databases, caches, load balancers), data flow, scaling strategies, and architectural trade-offs.
*   **Low-Level Design (LLD):** The zoomed-in view of specific features. It focuses on code-level details, including classes, function names, data structures, and object-oriented design.

## 2. Requirements Gathering

Before designing any system, you must define its requirements by asking five key questions:

## 3. Monolith vs. Microservices

*   **Monolith Architecture:** The entire application is bundled into a single codebase and deployed as one unit.
    *   *Pros:* Simple to build, test, deploy, and debug. Ideal for small teams and early-stage applications.

*   **Microservices Architecture:** The application is split into small, independent services (e.g., Feed Service, Upload Service, Notification Service), each with its own codebase and deployment cycle. Services communicate over the network.

## 4. Scaling Strategies

When a single server runs out of CPU and RAM, there are two ways to scale:

### Vertical Scaling (Scaling Up)
Adding more power (CPU, RAM, faster disks) to the existing server.
*   *Pros:* Simple; requires zero code changes.
*   *Cons:* Has a hard physical ceiling; cost increases exponentially for less performance gain at the high end; represents a single point of failure.

### Horizontal Scaling (Scaling Out)
Running multiple smaller, cheaper servers in parallel.
*   *Pros:* No upper limit; eliminates single points of failure.
*   *Cons:* Requires a load balancer and forces the application to be stateless.

### Stateless vs. Stateful Servers
*   **Stateful Servers:** Keep user data (like login sessions) in local memory. If a load balancer routes a user's subsequent request to a different server, the server will not recognize the user, forcing them to log out.
*   **Stateless Servers:** Keep no session data in local memory. All servers are disposable.
*   **The Fix:** Move session state out of individual servers into a fast, shared data store (e.g., Redis) that all servers can access.
*   *Note on Sticky Sessions:* This load balancer configuration routes a user to the same server every time. However, it defeats even load distribution and fails if that specific server dies.

# Building makemore: Character-Level Language Modeling

makemore is a character-level language model that generates text (such as name-like sequences) based on a training dataset. It treats text as a sequence of individual characters and predicts the next character in the sequence given the preceding context.

## The Bigram Language Model (Counting Approach)

A bigram model predicts the next character by looking only at the single immediate preceding character. It models local, two-character transitions.
